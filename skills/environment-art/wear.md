# Wear, damage and repair: what causes each and where it goes

Stone conservation has a closed vocabulary of damage in which almost every defect comes with a
cause, and so with a place. The first three columns follow the ICOMOS-ISCS glossary of stone
deterioration, structural writing on old walls, an archaeological survey of Pompeii's streets and
a conservation guide to historic paving, plus what game artists say about wear. The last column
is our own reading for a high camera and low-poly forms; treat it as a starting point.

| Defect | Real cause | Where it belongs | From a high camera, low-poly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rounded edge | Slow loss on exposed edges | Every long-exposed outer edge; most on wall tops, step fronts, kerbs | Bevel width, not noise: a wider bevel is older or more exposed |
| Chipped edge | Overload or knocks at block edges | Bearing points, column tops and bases, edges beside traffic, paving joints | One or two triangular notches a stone at most, on the traffic side |
| Knocked-off corner | Impact; the corner hits the ground first | Free corners of slabs, steps, plinths, coping stones | One clean diagonal cut. The best-reading defect at small size |
| Hairline cracks | Heat, fine fatigue | Thin parts, large sun-baked faces | Leave out: too fine to see |
| Fracture | Overload, a void under a slab, an iron clamp | Across a slab from edge to edge; near bearing points | A line that starts and ends at an edge or joint, a slight level change either side |
| Star crack, burst crater | A rusting or swelling metal fixing; an impact | Round sockets, clamps, hinge and ring fixings; at a strike | Short lines out from a dark socket, with a green or brown halo |
| Spalling | Salt under the surface; fire | The low band of walls; round old hearths | A shallow flat-bottomed patch with a hard outline, raw colour inside |
| Hollowed block | Salt and wind on a weak stone | Single weak blocks; the wall foot | One hollowed block in a run of sound ones |
| Missing stone | Robbing, collapse, displacement | Wall tops and ends, openings, round a failed lintel | A socket showing rubble core or shadow; the stone lies near, or is gone |
| Tilted or sunk slab | Lost bedding, subsidence | Low points, paving edges, beside collapses; in patches of a handful | Turn it a few degrees, drop one edge, open the joint on the high side |
| Bulge, lean, collapse | Lost joint stiffness, sliding, a failed foot | Bulge at mid-height; lean at the top; collapse from the top down | A breach with a ragged stepped profile and a fan of rubble on the side it fell to |
| Patch repair | Later upkeep | On the worst wear on the busiest routes, in the joints | Stone of another colour, size or bond; a filled joint; a metal or mortar plug |
| Polish and dishing | Feet, wheels, dragged loads | Thresholds, gate throats, stair centres, pinch points | A lighter band following the route; slab centres dished on the route only |
| Bleaching | Sun, loss of polish | Sun-facing and upward faces | Higher value, less saturation on tops; true colour in shade and under overhangs |
| Stain or streak | Metal corrosion, seeping water, fire | Straight below fittings; down from gaps in a wall top | A mark with its source visible at its top; short and tight where it is dry |
| Soot | Fire, lamps, hearths | Above openings and niches, on undersides | A dark fan rising from an opening and fading upward |
| Sand and dust drift | Wind and gravity | Inside corners, wall feet, steps, joints, the lee of obstacles, empty sockets | A wedge of sand colour climbing the wall foot; joints filled flush |
| Lichen, moss, plants | Damp and shade | The shady side, joints, cracks, near water | A few dry tufts at the shaded wall foot; none on open paving |
| Fallen debris | Gravity, from a named source above | Under the gap it came from, spreading outward | Largest blocks nearest the wall, small scatter furthest out |

## Old paving is a map of use

Seen from above, a paved yard records where people went. At Pompeii one junction lay on over 91%
of all routes across the city; three streets held 81% of all the iron repairs; 84% of those were
in deeply rutted stretches; and every solid repair piece sat in a joint between stones. So:

- Draw the routes first: gate to gate, gate to whatever stood in the middle, along the wall.
- Wear, polish and dishing follow the routes and peak where they cross or narrow.
- Nearly all repairs go on one or two routes, at the peaks.
- Thin flags crack and rock; small deep blocks sink together in patches of about ten, or unravel
  from a lost edge.
- A broken corner widens the joint beside it. Repairs show as a patch of different stone with
  joints of a different width.
- Upkeep is never finished: a ruin that was lived in has an unfinished job somewhere.

## A dry, sun-bleached place

Moss, long run-off streaks, black crust and climbing growth all need water. What keeps a cause in
a dry yard:

- **Mechanical damage:** knocked corners, fractures, breaches.
- **Abrasion:** routes polished pale, dished slabs at gates.
- **Bleaching:** tops lighter and greyer than sheltered faces.
- **Loss at the wall foot:** a raw band where wash or plaster has gone from the bottom 15 to 60 cm,
  and bare patches at hand and shoulder height beside doorways.
- **Sand:** in every low spot, joint and empty socket, and banked on the lee side of walls.
- **Soot:** above anywhere a fire burned.
- **Metal stain:** short and tight under a bronze or iron fitting. Bronze stains green.
- **Growth:** a few dry tufts in joints at the shaded wall foot, no more.

Fewer kinds of mark, each with a cause the player can see, suits a stylised game.

## How much (a proposal to test, not a sourced rule)

No source gives counts. Start here and adjust against the target picture:

- **Large** (changes a structure's outline: a breach, a fallen corner, a missing run of coping):
  one to three in a yard, each tied to an event in the history.
- **Medium** (changes one stone's outline: a knocked corner, a fracture, a tilted or missing stone,
  a patch): about one stone in five to eight inside a damage group, near none in rest areas. One
  geometric defect on a damaged stone, two on a hero stone. A corner cut takes a fifth to a third
  of an edge, so it reads as a shape.
- **Small** (colour only: bleaching, sand in joints, a stain, soot): by vertex colour or decal.
- Anything finer, as geometry, is left out.
- Defects thin out with distance from their cause: dense at the breach, gone a few stones away.
  The cluster points at where it began.

## The check a critic applies to a picture

1. Can a viewer point to the cause of each defect, or name it in a sentence?
2. Is every chip on a free edge or corner, and none in the middle of a face?
3. Does every crack begin and end at an edge, joint or crack, and change where it crosses a joint?
4. Does every stain and star crack have its source?
5. Does wear follow the routes, and do repairs sit where wear is worst?
6. Is sand only on horizontals, in corners, joints and sockets?
7. Is debris under the gap it fell from?
8. Is wall damage at the foot, top, corners and openings, not even up the face?
9. Is there at least one repair and one trace of present use?
10. Are there rest areas with no damage at all?
